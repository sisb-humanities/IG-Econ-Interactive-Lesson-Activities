Thailand

Rubber prices are high. Why can't farmers just make more?

Thailand grows about one third of the world's natural rubber. Farmers collect latex by tapping the bark of rubber trees.

2011Rubber prices hit a record. Farmers across Asia plant many new trees.
2015The new trees are ready. Too much rubber: Thai rubber sheet falls to about 37 baht/kg.
2026World supply is tight. Smoked rubber sheet reaches 97.90 baht/kg, the highest in ten years.
RememberA new rubber tree needs 5 to 7 years before it can be tapped.

Sources: Krungsri Research 2026; Bangkok Post, Dec 2015; Rubber Authority of Thailand via The Nation, 28 Oct 2026; FAO.

Think first

A farmer in Surat Thani sees the price of rubber rise from 60 to 90 baht per kg. What can she do this week to sell more?

Her rubber goes from 1,000 kg to 1,050 kg a month (+5%). The price went up by 50%. Which change was bigger?

So how responsive was her supply to the higher price?

Now it has a name

Economists divide the two percentages. This is price elasticity of supply (PES).

PES = %Δ quantity supplied ÷ %Δ price = (+5) ÷ (+50) = 0.1

0.1 is less than 1. Quantity supplied changed by a smaller percentage than price, so supply was price inelastic. Notice that PES is positive: price and quantity supplied move in the same direction.

Over time

What about the long run?

After the 2011 record price, farmers planted many new trees. What do you think happened when those trees could be tapped, years later?

Same story abroad: cocoa passed US$12,000 a tonne in December 2024 after disease and bad weather in West Africa. Trees cannot be replaced quickly. By early 2026 the price was back near US$3,000 as the crop recovered (FoodNavigator, March 2026).
